#### ABSTRACT

The main no-load power loss source of a rear axle is the pre-loaded pinion tapered roller bearings, as concluded in a previous work. Therefore, the pinion roller bearings torque loss was studied in detail, where the influence of several parameters was considered, such as, the starting torque of the pinion tapered roller bearings, the oil level, the lubricant viscosity and the rotational speed.

Due to the nature of the assembly of the pinion shaft assembly, pre-load in the tapered roller bearings is difficult to measure, however the starting torque of this assembly can be easily measured.

For this reason, in this work a rolling bearing torque loss empirical equation was developed, where it uses as input parameters the starting torque, the oil dynamic viscosity, operating speed and the rolling bearing mean diameter and width.

# **1. Introduction**

Energy efficiency in vehicles is becoming an increasingly important aspect, in order to reduce gas emissions and operating cost. The vehicle drivetrain is responsible for a significant part of the overall power loss, where one of its most relevant components, the axle box, features a low efficiency, between 90 and 93% [[1](#page-19-0)].

Therefore the power loss of a rear axle gear transmission has become an important field of investigation [\[2](#page-19-0)–8]. Some studies with tapered roller bearings lubricated with axle oils have been done [[9,10](#page-19-0)], aiming to analyse the influence of lubricant and operating conditions in the power loss of such rolling bearings.

A previous work [\[11](#page-19-0)] allowed to conclude, that the pinion tapered roller bearings are the main responsible for the rear axle no-load inefficiency, because of the high preload that they are subjected to, necessary to assure a high rigidity to the pinion gear set, as requested by the hypoid gear set.

The power loss in rolling bearings can be divided in two major parts, the load dependent and load independent losses.

The load dependent losses, which are outside of the focus of this work, occur due to the friction between contacting bodies of power transmitting components. For the rolling bearings, the load dependent losses depend on their type and size, on rolling and sliding conditions and on lubricant type [[12\]](#page-19-0).

The no-load losses occur due to the mechanical components immersed, partially or totally, in the oil sump, even without torque transmission. The torque loss generated by tapered roller bearings also depends on their size, on the sliding and rolling conditions and on the lubricant type.

Studies to the rolling bearings losses have already been done, aiming to develop models that can predict the torque losses, usually based on experimental results. The SKF [\[13](#page-19-0)] model is complex and considers the influence of a lot of parameters, being one of the most used models due to its reasonably good estimations and high versatility, although it is the more complex of the models. The ISO [\[14](#page-19-0)] model is simpler and depends on less parameters, mainly on the bearing mean diameter, lubricant viscosity, rotational speed, equivalent load and some coeficients, where its simplicity leads to less satisfactory results. In 1972 Witte [\[15](#page-19-0)] proposed a model for the estimation of the torque loss of axially loaded tapered roller bearings, a model that was specially design for the same type of bearings as in the current study. A theoretical equation for the friction torque loss was derived for this specific case of study, necessary to fundament some of the presented work. Since the current work also deals with axially loaded tapered rolling bearings Witte's model may be the model with more potential. All the models depend on the preload that the rolling bearings are subjected to, a variable difficult to measure.

To understand the influence of different operating conditions on the tapered roller bearings (no-load) torque loss, the tests were performed for large ranges of operating speed, of the oil sump temperature, of the preload applied to tapered roller bearings of the pinion shaft assembly and of oil sump level.

#### 3.2. Measurements at low speeds up to 750 rpm

#### 3.2.1. Temperature measurements at low speed up to 750 rpm

Fig. 7 shows the stabilized temperatures in the REB and Gear chambers, for operating speeds up to 750 rpm. Under these conditions, the oil sump temperature in the Gear chamber generally remains almost constant ( $\pm$  1.0 °C) independently of the operating speed and of the starting torque.

At these speeds, the oil sump temperature in the REB chamber is always lower that the temperature in the Gear chamber ( $T_{sump}^{REB} \leq T_{sump}^{Gear}$ ), but it increases progressively as the speed increases. However, for this speed range,  $T_{sump}^{REB}$  is almost independent of the starting torque.

These measurements show that it is not possible to keep the oil sump temperature constant in both chambers simultaneously.

#### 3.2.2. Torque loss measurements at low speed up to 750 rpm

In order to better understand the influence of the starting torque,  $M_{st}$ , on the pinion assembly, it was necessary to measure and analyse the torque loss measurements at low speeds (0  $\leq$  n  $\leq$  750 rpm), as shown in Fig. 8.

At very low speed ( $\omega < 3\pi\,\mathrm{rad/s}$ ) the torque loss decreases sharply as the speed increases, corresponding to what can be designated as a transient torque, corresponding to the transition from starting to dynamic conditions.

Simultaneously, as the speed increases ( $\omega > 3\pi$  rad/s, minimum speed allowed by the test machine) the torque loss increases monotonically with the speed, corresponding to a hydrodynamic behaviour.

The overall torque loss of the pinion assembly is the sum of these two components, transient and hydrodynamic, as shown schematically in Fig. 9. However, Fig. 8 also shows that the hydrodynamic torque depends on the starting torque, since higher starting torques always generate higher hydrodynamic torques, even in the operating speed range ( $500 \le n \le 2500 \text{ rpm}$ ) of the axle box.

At very low speeds, it is possible to observe a clear decrease of the transient torque, up to a point the hydrodynamic torque becomes similar to the transient torque.

#### *3.3.2. Torque loss measurements in the range 500*–*2500 rpm*

The normal range of the operating speed of the axle box is between 500 and 2500 rpm, corresponding to vehicle speeds between 20 and 100 km/h.

[Fig. 11](#page-7-0) shows the influence of the input speed on the torque loss of the pinion assembly, for three different oil sump temperatures and three different starting torques (corresponding to three different rolling bearing pre-loads). As expected, when the speed increases the torque loss also increases towards an almost asymptotic value at 2000 rpm. In addition, when the oil sump temperature increases, the oil viscosity decreases and the torque loss becomes smaller.

The influence of the starting torque (or pre-load) is also very significant. For an oil sump temperature of 62.2 ◦C (*υ* = 42.9 cSt) and an input speed of 2000 rpm, the torque loss of the pinion assembly increases from 1.35 Nm up to 1.60 Nm (+18%) when the starting torque is creased from 0.75 Nm to 1.90 Nm. When the starting torque was increased to 3.00 Nm the additional increase of the torque loss of the pinion assembly is smaller.

For low temperature and high speed (e.g. *TGear sump* = 50 ◦C and 2000 ≤ n ≤ 2500 rpm) the torque loss stabilizes or even decreases slightly. In fact, although the oil sump temperature is kept constant, locally (e.g. inside the rolling bearings), the mass temperature might rise above the oil sump temperature and consequently the viscosity drops and the torque loss decreases. The results were obtained considering the mass temperature of the bearing races is equal rolling bearing chamber temperature.

The results shown in [Fig. 11](#page-7-0) clearly indicate that the starting torque associated with the rolling bearings pre-load has a very significant influence on the pinion assembly torque loss.

#### 5.2. Starting torque

The starting torque is the sum of the starting torques resulting from each one of the tapered roller bearings, that is,

$$M_{st} = M_{st1} + M_{st2} ag{12}$$

According to the SKF [13] rolling bearing friction torque model, at the onset of movement, the starting torque corresponds to the sliding friction torque causing boundary lubricant conditions ( $M_{st} = M_{sl}$ ), since the remaining rolling bearing friction torque components ( $M_{rr}$ , rolling torque and  $M_{drag}$ , drag torque), which are dependent on the rolling bearing speed, are negligible.

The relations between the starting torque and the axial preload in each tapered roller bearing are established by the following expressions:

$$M_{st1} = M_{sl1} = \mu_{bl} \cdot S_1 \cdot d_m^{0.82} \cdot S_2 \cdot Y \cdot F_a$$
 (13)

And

$$M_{st2} = M_{sl2} = \mu_{bl} \cdot S_2 \cdot d_{m2}^{0.82} \cdot S_2 \cdot Y \cdot F_a$$
 (14)

Since the total starting torque is measured and known, the geometric constants S1 and S2 are known from the SKF catalogue, and the mean diameter of each tapper roller bearing is known, it is possible to determine the corresponding axial preload, Fa.

Using the presented equation system (15).

$$\begin{cases} F_a \simeq \frac{M_{st1}}{\mu_{bl} \cdot S_1 \cdot d_{m1}^{0.82} \cdot S_2 \cdot Y} \\ F_a \simeq \frac{M_{st2}}{\mu_{bl} \cdot S_1 \cdot d_{m2}^{0.82} \cdot S_2 \cdot Y} \\ M_{st} = M_{st1} + M_{st2} \end{cases}$$
(15)

Using the system of equation (15), the following preloads were estimated: 2083 N at 0.75 Nm, 5279 N at 1.9 Nm and 8336 N at 3 Nm. These preloads are necessary to estimate the roller bearings torque loss using SKF [13], ISO [14] and Witte [15] models.

#### 6.1. An empiric equation for the tapered R.B. torque loss

As thoroughly shown in Appendix A, the tapered roller bearings torque loss is the sum of two components, the transient torque loss and the hydrodynamic torque loss, both depending on the starting torque generated by the pre-load applied to the tapered roller bearing assembly. The transient torque loss is given by equation (16). It depends on the starting torque and decreases very rapidly as the speed increases, as shown in Fig. 9.

$$M_{transient} = M_{st} \cdot e^{-\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}} \tag{16}$$

The hydrodynamic torque is given by equation (17). It also depends on the starting torque, as shown by the experimental measurements shown on Figs. 8 and 14, and on the dynamic viscosity of the oil, on the operating speed, on the Reynolds number and on the tapper rolling bearings dimensions (*dm* and T). The Reynolds number is used, so the viscous and inertial behaviours of the lubricant are also considered, that is included in the dimensionless coefficient Cm, with the oil level high and the rolling bearing mean radius.

$$M_{hydrodynamic} = \left(M_{st} + C_1\right)^{\alpha} \cdot \left[ \left(\eta \cdot \omega \cdot dm_1^2 \cdot T_1\right)^{1-\alpha} \cdot Cm_1 + \left(\eta \cdot \omega \cdot dm_2^2 \cdot T_2\right)^{1-\alpha} \cdot Cm_2 \right]$$
(17)

where the dimensionless constant  $Cm_i$  is given by equation (18).

$$Cm_i = C_2 \cdot \left(\frac{h}{R_m}\right)^{\beta} \cdot Re_r^{\theta} \quad , i = 1, 2$$

$$\tag{18}$$

The values of the exponents  $\alpha$ ,  $\beta$  and  $\theta$ , as well as the values of the constants  $C_1$ ,  $C_2$  and  $\omega_0$  were determined by correlation with the experimental measurements:  $\alpha = 0.45$ ,  $\beta = 0.28$ ,  $\theta = -0.080$ ,  $C_1 = 1.86$ ,  $C_2 = 56.3$  and  $\omega_0 = 2$  rad s.<sub>1</sub>. The Reynolds number is defined according equation (19).

$$Re_r = \frac{R_m^2 \cdot \omega}{R} \tag{19}$$

Equation (20) shows the torque loss of the tapper roller bearing assembly. A comparison between equation (20) and the experimental results is shown in Fig. 18, where a correlation factor of 0.963 was found.

$$T_{VL0} = M_{st} e^{-\frac{\omega}{\omega_0}} + (M_{st} + C_1)^{\alpha} \cdot \left[ \left( \eta \cdot \omega \cdot dm_1^2 \cdot T_1 \right)^{1-\alpha} \cdot Cm_1 + \left( \eta \cdot \omega \cdot dm_2^2 \cdot T_2 \right)^{1-\alpha} \cdot Cm_2 \right]$$
(20)

Fig. 19 compares the experimental measurements and the new equation estimations for the torque loss of the tapered roller bearings assembly for an oil sump temperature of 77.1 °C (v=27.7 cSt) and for the three starting torques tested. Similarly good correlations were obtained for all the other operating temperatures, from 50 °C up to 90 °C.

For the moment, equation (20) has only been verified for the tandem tapered roller bearings of the axle box (ref.33107518807) shown in Table 2.

# **7. Conclusions**

The importance of the vehicle efficiency is increasing, being the rolling bearings one of main aspects that can be improved. So, in this work, the tapered roller bearings of a rear axle were studied, where the influence of pre-load, rotational speed, lubricant viscosity and oil level were analysed in the friction torque. This study allowed to better understand the power loss in the rolling bearings and to compare the estimations presented by some models found in the literature.

The experimental results allowed to conclude that a starting torque increase leads to a torque loss increase, until a certain value, where it remains constant, effect explained by the pre-load increase and the hydrodynamic behaviour at higher speeds. As expected, the lubricant viscosity and rotational speed increase led to a torque loss increase. A high torque loss is verified at the starting of the movement of the rolling bearings, that decreases very rapidly down to a value where it increases again. The lubricant level decrease induces a torque loss decrease down to a certain point, where the bearings lubricant starvation starts to be verified.

The experimental results allowed to perform a comparison between the main models presented in the literature, where some discrepancies where verified, mainly due to models reaction to the operating conditions variation. The Witte model has shown the better estimations when compared with the experimental results, although any model has shown a suitable reaction to the pre-load increase.

An empirical equation capable of estimating the rolling bearings torque loss was developed, based on the experimental results, where instead of the preload, the starting torque was considered. The developed equation is based on a theoretical model, that was developed and explained.

To conclude, the developed equation was analysed, where a good correlation with the experimental results was obtained. To assure a good behaviour of the presented equation, a sensibility analysis was performed, where the presented equation reacted similarly to the SKF and Witte models.

#### **Appendix A. Tapered roller bearing (TRB) torque loss - Houpert's model**

Houpert [\[20](#page-19-0)] developed a theoretical model for the torque loss in tapered roller bearings, represented by equations (A.1) and (A.2).

$$dT = dT_{FR} + dT_{rib} + dT_{MER} \tag{A.1}$$

$$dT = 2 \cdot (FR_o + FR_i) \cdot \frac{R_o \cdot R_i}{d_m} + \frac{Mrib \cdot R_o}{d} + \frac{MER_i \cdot R_o + MER_o \cdot R_i}{d}$$
(A.2)

This equation gives the torque acting on each rolling element, which depends on the hydrodynamic resistant forces (*FR*) in the inner and outer raceways, on the elastic rolling moment (*MER*) and on the RIB torque (*Mrib*).

Equation (A.3) is derived from the previous equation by replacing the outer (*Ro*) and inner (*Ri*) race radius of the bearing by some approximations based on the bearing mean diameter and the constant *γ*. So, the rolling element torque is expressed for each element in classical parameters (rolling bearing geometrical parameters, axial load and friction coefficients), considering only the inner ring rotation,

$$dT = 0.5 \cdot dm \cdot (1 + \gamma) \cdot (1 - \gamma) \cdot (FR_o + FR_i) + 0.212 \cdot Z \cdot ((1 + \gamma)(Mrib + MER_i) + (1 - \gamma) \cdot MER_o)$$
(A.3)

where 
$$\gamma = \frac{d \cdot \cos \alpha^*}{d_m}$$
.

# *Appendix A.1. Hydrodynamic Race Torque*

Using the displacement of pressure center expression, presented by Hamrock [\[21](#page-19-0)], Houpert [[22\]](#page-19-0) derived the rolling traction force for a line contact under full-film EHD lubrication, given by equation [\(A.4\)](#page-15-0),

<span id="page-15-0"></span>
$$FR = 0.04 \cdot E' \cdot R_x \cdot 1 \cdot U^{0.44} \cdot W^{0.37}$$
(A.4)

where E' is the equivalent Young's modulus,  $R_x$  is the equivalent radius in the rolling direction, 1 is the roller length, U is a dimensionless speed parameter and W is a dimensionless load.

The rolling traction force expression can be expressed in classical parameters, as presented in equations (A.5) and (A.6), for the inner race (i) and outer race (o) contacts, respectively.

$$FR_{i} = 0.0165 \cdot E^{\prime 0.19} \cdot Z^{-0.56} \cdot dm^{0.63} \cdot l^{0.63} \cdot (1+\gamma)^{-0.19} \cdot \left(\eta \cdot \omega_{i} \cdot (1-\gamma^{2})\right)^{0.44} \cdot \left(F_{a} \cdot \sin \alpha^{*}\right)^{0.37}$$
(A.5)

$$FR_o = 0.0165 \cdot E^{\prime 0.19} \cdot Z^{-0.56} \cdot dm^{0.63} \cdot l^{0.63} \cdot (1 - \gamma)^{-0.19} \cdot \left( \eta \cdot \omega_{i} \cdot (1 - \gamma^2) \right)^{0.44} \cdot \left( F_a \cdot \sin \alpha^* \right)^{0.37}$$
(A.6)

where the roller diameter and the normal force approximations were considered, as expressed in equations (A.7) and (A.8).

$$d \approx \frac{\pi \cdot dm}{1.33 \cdot Z} \tag{A.7}$$

$$Q \approx \frac{F_a \cdot \sin \alpha^*}{7} \tag{A.8}$$

The rolling traction force can be converted in torque using equation (A.3), obtaining equation (A.9).

$$dT_{FR} = 0.5 \cdot dm \cdot (1 + \gamma) \cdot (1 - \gamma) \cdot (FR_o + FR_i) \tag{A.9}$$

$$= 0.00825 \cdot (1-\gamma) \cdot (1+\gamma) \cdot \left(1-\gamma^2\right)^{0.44} \cdot \left((1-\gamma)^{-0.19} + (1+\gamma)^{-0.19}\right) \cdot Z^{-0.56} \cdot \sin\!\alpha^*$$

$$\cdot E'^{0.19} \cdot dm^{1.63} \cdot l^{0.63} \cdot (\eta \cdot \omega_i)^{0.44} \cdot F_a^{0.37}$$

$$=0.00825 \cdot \gamma^* \cdot \sin \alpha^* \cdot Z^{-0.56} \cdot E'^{0.19} \cdot dm^{1.63} \cdot l^{0.63} \cdot (\eta \cdot \omega_i)^{0.44} \cdot F_a^{0.37}$$

where  $\gamma^*$  is given by equation (A.10).

$$\gamma^* = \left(1 - \gamma\right) \cdot \left(1 + \gamma\right) \cdot \left(1 - \gamma^2\right)^{0.44} \cdot \left[ (1 - \gamma)^{-0.19} + (1 + \gamma)^{-0.19} \right] \tag{A.10}$$

#### Appendix A.2. RIB Torque

The contact between each roller and the rib results in a moment, expressed in (A.11), characteristic of tapered roller bearings, which depends only on the rolling bearing geometry.

$$Mrib \approx \mu_{rib} \cdot Q_{rib} \cdot f \sqrt{1 + 0.18 \left(\frac{b}{f}\right)^2}$$
 (A.11)

Similar to the rolling traction force, the Mrib can be expressed in classical rolling bearings parameters, as given by equation A.12.

$$Mrib \approx 2 \cdot \mu_{rib} \left( \frac{F_a \cdot \sin \alpha^*}{Z} \right) \sin \nu \cdot f \cdot \sqrt{1 + 0.18 \left( \frac{b}{f} \right)}$$
 (A.12)

where  $\nu$  is half the included roller angle, of the order of  $2^{\circ}$ , f is the height of the roller - rib contact, b is half of the rolling element - race contact and  $\mu_{rib}$  is the local friction coefficient.

After applying the ratio between dimensions, as expressed in equation (A.3), equation (A.13) is obtained, where the geometric parameters were replaced by the variable B and divided in both the transient and the EHD regime, using a weighting factor  $\phi_{bl}$  similar to the one presented by the SKF [13].

$$dT_{rib} = 0.212 \cdot Z \cdot (1 + \gamma) \cdot Mrib = 0.424 \cdot (1 + \gamma) \cdot \sqrt{1 + 0.18 \cdot \left(\frac{b}{f}\right)^2} \cdot \sin \nu \cdot \sin \alpha^* \cdot \mu_{rib} \cdot F_a \cdot f = 0.424 \cdot B \cdot \sin \alpha^* \cdot \mu_{rib} \cdot f \cdot F_a$$

$$= 0.424 \cdot B \cdot \phi_{rib} \cdot f \cdot \sin \alpha^* \cdot \mu_{bl} \cdot F_a + 0.424 \cdot B \cdot (1 - \phi_{rib}) \cdot f \cdot \sin \alpha^* \cdot \mu_{EHD} \cdot F_a$$
(A.13)

where the variable B and the RIB friction coefficient  $\mu_{rib}$  are given by equations (A.14) and (A.15), respectively.

$$B = \left(1 + \gamma\right) \cdot \sin\nu \cdot \sqrt{1 + 0.18 \cdot \left(\frac{b}{f}\right)} \tag{A.14}$$

$$\mu_{rib} = \phi_{rib} \cdot \mu_{bl} + (1 - \phi_{rib}) \cdot \mu_{FHD}$$
 (A.15)

$$MER = 0.00042 \cdot Q \cdot R_x \cdot \frac{\mu_e}{0.11}$$
 (A.16)

When expressed in classical parameters, the equations (A.17) and (A.18) are obtained,

$$MER_i = 0.004509 \cdot \frac{\sin\alpha^*}{Z^2} \cdot F_a \cdot dm \cdot (1+\gamma)^{-1} \cdot \mu_e$$
(A.17)

$$MER_o = 0.004509 \cdot \frac{\sin\alpha^*}{Z^2} \cdot F_a \cdot dm \cdot (1 - \gamma)^{-1} \cdot \mu_e$$
(A.18)

Where *μe*is the equivalent friction coefficient.

According to equation (A.3), the final elastic rolling moment is calculated using equation (A.19).

$$+0.001912 \cdot \frac{\sin\alpha^*}{\sigma} \cdot dm \cdot \phi_{mer} \cdot \mu_{bl} \cdot F_a$$

Equation (A.20) clearly shows that the torque acting on each roller element depends on the TRB geometry (Z, *γ*\*, *α*\*, l, *dm*, b, f), on the lubricant dynamic viscosity (*η*), on the inner raceway speed (*ωi*), on the axial load (*Fa*) and on the coefficient of friction (*ϕRIB*, *ϕmer*, *μbl*, *μEHD*).

Regarding the roller element torque loss components, the transient and the hydrodynamic regimes can be easily distinguished, as the transient regimes tend to start at a defined torque loss and progressively decrease to zero as the speed increases, while the hydrodynamic losses start from zero and increase continuously with the rotational speed.
