# Micro-geometry effects on the sliding friction transition in elastohydrodynamic lubrication

G E Morales-Espejel1,2∗, AWWemekamp1, and A Félix-Quiñonez

<sup>1</sup>SKF Engineering and Research Centre B.V., Nieuwegein, The Netherlands

<sup>2</sup>INSA-Lyon, CNRS, Université de Lyon, LaMCoS UMR5259, France

The manuscript was received on 20 January 2010 and was accepted after revisionfor publication on 10 March 2010.

DOI: 10.1243/13506501JET787

Abstract: An engineering scheme is proposed to calculate the effects of deterministic microgeometry in sliding friction for the transition between full-film elastohydrodynamic and boundary lubrication regimes. The method combines the concepts of micro-geometry deformation from lubricated and/or dry contact conditions. Pressures are calculated from the elasto-plastic displacements and the load sharing between lubricant and contacts is resolved iteratively in the classical way, i.e. flow balance. Thus, by varying the lubricant entrainment speed in the contact for known values of boundary and full-film friction coefficients, it is then possible to calculate the Stribeck curve. The scheme is semi-analytical and the use of fast Fourier techniques makes it attractive for engineering applications. Theoretical predictions are compared with numerical solutions from the literature and with in-house measurements for different surface topographies and the results are in reasonable agreement with the model.

Keywords: Stribeck curves, sliding friction, roughness, EHL

## 1 INTRODUCTION

The prediction of friction in elastohydrodynamically lubricated (EHL) contacts remains a challenge for engineers because of many unresolved issues. One of them is the understanding of rheological aspects of the lubricant in the conditions of an EHL contact, despite the progress already made [1]. Another aspect modifying friction is the boundary layer bounded to the surface of the contact. This layer provides the surface with lubricity and reduces substantially typical dry contact friction coefficients, i.e. 0.5–0.8 to more modest values in the order of 0.1–0.15. The properties of this layer and therefore its effects on friction depend largely on the chemistry lubricantadditive surface. They are difficult to predict, and in engineering applications, they are mainly measured.

The final important aspect that considerably influences rolling and sliding friction in EHL contacts is the surface topography. Even in an ideal situation where all rheological and boundary layer aspects of the surface-lubricant couple are well known, the calculation of the surface topography effects on friction is still a challenge. The greatest influence is generally found in the transition region from boundary to fullfilm regime. This is the subject of the present paper, which is limited to sliding friction and EHL conditions.

Pioneering experimental work on partial lubrication is due to Tallian et al. [2, 3]. Johnson et al. [4] used the stochastic asperity contact model from Greenwood and Williamson [5] to determine the load balance between the lubricant and the dry contacts. Although, Johnson et al. were only interested on film thickness effects and contact time, the idea was widely used later by many authors for the calculation of friction in partial lubrication regime [6, 7]. Deterministic modelling of partial lubrication has been carried out by several researchers, e.g. Jiang et al. [8], Redlich et al. [9], Wang et al. [10], and more recently Deolalikar et al. [11].

In the present article, it is not the intention of the authors to address the basic physical phenomena of the partial-lubrication regime (e.g. film breakdown, lubricant micro-flow, droplet formation, surface energy effects, etc.), which are fundamental in the understanding and proper modelling of film formation and friction in this regime and would certainly prevent the authors from the use of the Reynolds equation among other things [12]. The objective of the present paper is only to present a fast engineering model capable of capturing the average behaviour of different deterministic surface micro-geometries in the contribution of sliding friction and load carrying aspects of the lubricant and the asperities, once the friction coefficients in the boundary and full-film regimes are known.

The approach used in the present work is based on the assumption of one nominally flat elastic–perfectly plastic rough surface in ‘contact’ with a smooth flat rigid counterpart. This approach allows for the simplification of the geometry and assumes that the mean film thickness and pressure are known (as in the central location of a contact). Therefore, only clearance and pressure fluctuations are calculated. The inlet and outlet of the contact are not fully included in the analysis. For the ‘dry’ contact patches, a contact model with a fast Fourier transform (FFT) approach, as described in Stanley and Kato [13], is used but extended to three-dimensional (3D) geometries. Furthermore, the perfectly plastic behaviour of the asperities at high pressures is introduced. For the lubricated areas an upgraded version ofthe so-called ‘amplitudereduction’ approach described in reference [14] will be justified and used. Finally, the sharing of the load between asperities and lubricant is calculated by using the classical iterative approach of flow balance as described initially by Johnson et al. [4].

The final model avoids the expensive full numerical calculations from previous models. The normalized friction results calculated for real measured surfaces with the present model are compared with experimental measurements from a ball-on-disc machine. The results are encouraging and show that this simple approach is able to capture the overall behaviour of the sliding friction in the transition regime.

Since thermal effects are not included, the model is limited to low sliding values, e.g. raceway contacts in rolling bearings.

## 2 DRY CONTACT MODEL

Stanley and Kato [13] used the work from Kalker [15] to solve the frictionless elastic contact problem, where the equilibrium solution has an equivalent variational statement, namely, find the pressures p to solve

$$
\begin{array} { l } { \operatorname* { m i n } ( f ) = \displaystyle \frac { 1 } { 2 } \int _ { S } p u ( p ) { \bf d } S + \int _ { S } p g { \bf d } S } \\ { \displaystyle \frac { 1 } { A } \int _ { S } p { \bf d } S = p _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t } } \quad p \geqslant 0 } \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $f$ is the total complementary energy, u the normal displacement and g the gap between the rigid plane and the undeformed elastic surface. The inequality in equation (1) means that negative pressures are not allowed and the last part of this equation imposes the prescribed load as a mean or target pressure $p _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t } } .$ . It follows the discrete representation of the statement

$$
\begin{array} { l } { \displaystyle \operatorname* { m i n } ( f ) = \frac { 1 } { 2 } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } p _ { i } u _ { i } + \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } p _ { i } g _ { i } } \\ { \displaystyle p _ { i } \geqslant 0 , \quad i = 1 , 2 , \ldots , n } \\ { \displaystyle \frac { 1 } { n } \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } p _ { i } = p _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t } } } \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

## 2.1 Algorithm for elastic dry contact

The numerical algorithm employed to find the pressures from equation (2) is also described in reference [13] and is as follows. Beginning with a guess for the matrix p which meets the equality and inequality constraints (a uniform pressure $\mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t } }$ is convenient), then

1. Calculate a candidate pressure matrix $\mathbf { p } ^ { \prime } = \mathbf { p } -$ grad $\{ f ( \mathbf { p } ) \}$ . In general, p will violate the constraints. grad $\{ f ( \mathbf { p } ) \} = \mathbf { u ( p ) } + \mathbf { g }$ , forf quadratic.

2. Shift p uniformly up or down so that the sum ofthe positive pressures equals the target load.

3. Truncate all $p _ { i } ^ { \prime } < 0 .$ , Thus, p meets all constraints.

4. Set $ { \mathbf { p } } =  { \mathbf { p } } ^ { \prime }$ , and repeat until convergence.

## 2.2 Modification for elasto-perfectly plastic material

The above algorithm is modified according to the scheme of Tian and Bushman [16] to introduce the perfectly plastic behaviour of the material. Here it is assumed that the region of plastic deformation is only confined within a very small area, it does not significantly alter the geometry of the elastically deformed contact surface. A contact point deforms plastically once the local contact pressure exceeds the hardness of the material $( p _ { \mathrm { l i m } } )$ . For elastic–perfectly plastic materials, the internal complementary energy is equal to the elastic strain energy; therefore, equation (1) remains valid when the plastic displacements added energy is not very large (small plastic deformations).

Thus, the elastic scheme can be slightly modified.

1. The elastic algorithm (as above) is started. However, after the calculation of pressures (step 1) at every iteration it follows the next steps 2 to 6 instead.

2. The elastic pressures are limited to a threshold value related to the material yield limit by $p _ { \mathrm { l i m } } \approx 1 . 8 \sigma _ { \mathrm { y i e l d } } .$

3. Truncate all $p _ { i } ^ { \prime } < 0 .$

4. Calculate $\Delta \mathbf { p } = \mathbf { p ^ { \prime } } - \mathbf { p } ,$ p being the pressure from the previous iteration.

5. Check for convergence mean $\{ \Delta \mathbf { p } \} / \mathbf { p } _ { \mathrm { t a r g e t } } = \mathrm { e r r o r } ,$ and repeat until convergence.

6. Go back to 3 until convergence.

The plastic displacements can be calculated by simply subtracting the elastic displacements from the total displacements.

## 2.3 Elastic displacements calculation

The numerical schemes described above require the calculation of the elastic normal displacements for a given normal surface pressure. Here the FFT method described in reference [16] will be followed, therefore

$$
\mathbf { u } ( \mathbf { p } ) = \mathrm { I F F T } \left\{ \mathbf { w } \cdot F F T ( \mathbf { p } ) \right\}\tag{3}
$$

where IFFT means inverse FFT, and w FFT(p) refers to element-by-element multiplication, w is a matrix containing numerical factors and it is known as the frequency response function. To calculate w, consider the displacement solution for a bi-sinusoidal pressure distribution as given by Johnson et al. [17]

$$
\begin{array} { l } { \displaystyle p ( x , y ) = \cos \left( \frac { n 2 \pi x } { L _ { x } } \right) \cos \left( \frac { m 2 \pi y } { L _ { y } } \right) } \\ { \displaystyle u ( x , y ) = \frac 2 { ( n ^ { 2 } + m ^ { 2 } ) E ^ { * } } \cos \left( \frac { n 2 \pi x } { \mathrm { L } _ { x } } \right) \cos \left( \frac { m 2 \pi y } { \mathrm { L } _ { y } } \right) } \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

From which the frequency response matrix is

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { w ( i , j ) = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { ( i - 1 ) ^ { 2 } + [ ( j - 1 ) ! ] ^ { 2 } } } , } \\ & { \quad \forall i = 1 , \ldots , n / 2 \quad \mathrm { a n d } \ j = 1 , \ldots , m / 2 } \\ & { \quad w ( i , j ) = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { ( i - 1 ) ^ { 2 } + [ ( m - j + 1 ) ! ] ^ { 2 } } } , } \\ & { \quad \forall i = 1 , \ldots , n / 2 \quad \mathrm { a n d } \ j = m / 2 + 1 , \ldots , m } \\ & { \quad w ( i , j ) = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { ( m - i + 1 ) ^ { 2 } + [ ( j - 1 ) ! ] ^ { 2 } } } , } \\ & { \quad \forall i = m / 2 + 1 , \ldots , n / 2 \quad \mathrm { a n d } \ j = 1 , \ldots , m / 2 } \\ & { \quad w ( i , j ) = \frac { 1 } { \sqrt { ( m - i + 1 ) ^ { 2 } + [ ( m - j + 1 ) ! ] ^ { 2 } } } , } \\ & { \quad \forall i = n / 2 + 1 , \ldots , n / 2 \quad \mathrm { a n d } \ j = m / 2 + 1 , \ldots , m } \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

for w(1, 1) 0, where $l = L _ { x } / L _ { y }$

## 3 LUBRICATION MODEL

A 3D model for the pressures and clearances induced by low-amplitude micro-geometry in EHL conditions and pure rolling is described in Morales-Espejel et al. [14]. This model is based on the transverse-roughness model described in Greenwood and Morales-Espejel [18] and modified for the longitudinal roughness components of pressure and clearances by using the amplitude reduction curve for isotropic waviness from Venner and Lubrecht [19]. Recently, a truly 3D model which also includes rolling-sliding with an Eyring fluid (non-Newtonian behaviour) has been proposed by Hooke [20] and Hooke et al. [21]. The present model is largely based on this scheme but some modifications have been introduced for the calculation of the amplitude of the complementary function. The general model for rolling–sliding used in the present paper is described as follows.

The Reynolds equation is

$$
\frac { \partial } { \partial x } \left( \frac { \rho h ^ { 3 } } { 1 2 \eta _ { x } } \frac { \partial p } { \partial x } \right) + \frac { \partial } { \partial y } \left( \frac { \rho h ^ { 3 } } { 1 2 \eta _ { y } } \frac { \partial p } { \partial y } \right) = \bar { u } \frac { \partial ( \rho h ) } { \partial x } + \frac { \partial ( \rho h ) } { \partial t }\tag{6}
$$

For an analysis at the centre of the EHL contact, considering a sinusoidal waviness of amplitude $r _ { \mathrm { a } }$ moving along x with speed $u _ { 2 }$

$$
\delta r = r _ { \mathrm { { a } } } \ \exp ( i \omega _ { x } x ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y )\tag{7}
$$

where $\omega _ { x } = 2 \pi / \lambda _ { x }$ and $\omega _ { y } = 2 \pi / \lambda _ { y }$

As the surfaces move, hydrodynamic pressures will be generated. Provided that the amplitude of the roughness is low, such pressures will be sinusoidal and will have the same wavelength – albeit not necessarily the same phase – as the undeformed roughness. Thus, they can be expressed as

$$
\delta p = p _ { \mathrm { a } } \exp ( i \omega _ { x } x ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y )\tag{8}
$$

where the imaginary part of the complex representation of the fluctuations $\delta p$ is due to the differences in magnitude and phase between the original surface and the pressure fluctuation. These pressures will deform the elastic surface creating elastic displacements on both surfaces; they can be added and written as

$$
\delta \nu = \nu _ { \mathrm { a } } \exp ( i \omega _ { x } x ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y )\tag{9}
$$

where the relationship between displacement and pressure amplitude is given by Johnson et al. [17]

$$
\nu _ { \mathrm { a } } = \frac { 4 p _ { \mathrm { a } } } { E _ { \sqrt [ ] { \omega _ { x } ^ { 2 } + \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } } } }\tag{10}
$$

The surface deformation is superimposed on the original roughness to produce the clearance variation inside the contact

$$
\delta h = h _ { \mathrm { a } } \exp ( i \omega _ { x } x ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y )\tag{11}
$$

where $h _ { \mathrm { a } } = r _ { \mathrm { a } } + \nu _ { \mathrm { a } }$

The pressure ripple will also change the density of the lubricant, the change may be written as

$$
\delta \rho = \rho _ { \mathrm { a } } \exp ( i \omega _ { x } x ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y )\tag{12}
$$

where $\rho _ { \mathrm { a } }$ is related to the pressure variation by

$$
\rho _ { \mathrm { a } } = \left( { \frac { \rho } { B } } \right) p _ { \mathrm { a } }\tag{13}
$$

with B being the bulk modulus of the lubricant at a given pressure, $\mathrm { d } \rho / \mathrm { d } p = \rho / B$

Now, since the amplitude of the variations is small, let us assume that the products of fluctuations can be neglected in the Reynolds equation (6) as well as products of derivatives; therefore, the Reynolds equation can be written as

$$
\begin{array} { c } { { \displaystyle \frac { h ^ { 3 } } { 1 2 } \left( \frac { 1 } { \eta _ { x } } \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } p } { \partial x ^ { 2 } } + \frac { 1 } { \eta _ { y } } \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } p } { \partial y ^ { 2 } } \right) } } \\ { { = \displaystyle \frac { 1 } { \rho } \left( \bar { u } h \frac { \partial \rho } { \partial x } + \bar { u } \rho \frac { \partial h } { \partial x } + h \frac { \partial \rho } { \partial t } + \rho \frac { \partial h } { \partial t } \right) } } \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

since $\begin{array} { r } { \mathrm { d } \rho / \mathrm { d } p = \rho / B , } \end{array}$ notice that

$$
{ \begin{array} { l } { { \frac { 1 } { \rho } } { \frac { \partial \rho } { \partial x } } = { \frac { 1 } { B } } { \frac { \partial p } { \partial x } } } \\ { { \frac { 1 } { \rho } } { \frac { \partial \rho } { \partial t } } = { \frac { 1 } { B } } { \frac { \partial p } { \partial t } } } \end{array} }\tag{15}
$$

therefore,

$$
\begin{array} { c } { { \displaystyle { \frac { h ^ { 3 } } { 1 2 } } \left( \frac { 1 } { \eta _ { x } } \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } p } { \partial x ^ { 2 } } + \frac { 1 } { \eta _ { y } } \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } p } { \partial y ^ { 2 } } \right) } } \\ { { = \bar { u } \frac { \partial h } { \partial x } + \frac { \partial h } { \partial t } + \frac { h } { B } \left( \bar { u } \frac { \partial p } { \partial x } + \frac { \partial p } { \partial t } \right) } } \end{array}\tag{16}
$$

Greenwood and Morales-Espejel [18, 22] have shown that the solution of the moving roughness problem is made oftwo components: the particular integral (moving steady-state) travelling with the speed of the rough surface $u _ { 2 }$ and a complementary function describing a propagated wave generated at the inlet, travelling at the average speed for the lubricant $\bar { u } .$

## 3.1 Particular integral

Pressure, clearance, and density variations can be added to the smooth contact and substituted in equation (16), then the equation simplifies to

$$
{ \frac { \rho h ^ { 3 } \omega _ { x } ^ { 2 } } { 1 2 \eta _ { x } } } p _ { \mathrm { a } } + { \frac { \rho h ^ { 3 } \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } } { 1 2 \eta _ { y } } } p _ { \mathrm { a } } = i ( u _ { 2 } - \bar { u } ) \omega _ { x } \left( \rho h _ { \mathrm { a } } + { \frac { \rho h } { B } } p _ { \mathrm { a } } \right)\tag{17}
$$

solving for $p _ { \mathrm { a } }$ equation (17) leads to

$$
\frac { p _ { \mathrm { a } } } { r _ { \mathrm { a } } } = \frac { \kappa E ^ { \prime } } { 4 } \frac { i Q } { 1 - i Q - i C Q }\tag{18}
$$

where $\kappa = \sqrt { \omega _ { x } ^ { 2 } + \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } } , C = h E ^ { \prime } \kappa / ( 4 B )$ and

$$
Q = \frac { 4 8 ( u _ { 2 } - \bar { u } ) \omega _ { x } } { E ^ { \prime } h ^ { 3 } \kappa [ ( \omega _ { x } ^ { 2 } / \eta _ { x } ) + ( \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } / \eta _ { y } ) ] }
$$

For an Eyring fluid $\dot { \gamma } = ( \tau _ { 0 } / \eta )$ sinh $( \tau / \tau _ { 0 } )$ the effective viscosities [23] are

$$
\begin{array} { c } { { \eta _ { x } = \displaystyle \frac { \eta } { \cosh ( \tau _ { m } / \tau _ { 0 } ) } } } \\ { { \eta _ { y } = \displaystyle \frac { \eta ( \tau _ { m } / \tau _ { 0 } ) } { \sinh ( \tau _ { m } / \tau _ { 0 } ) } } } \end{array}\tag{19}
$$

with $\tau _ { m }$ being the mean shear stress.

## 3.2 Complementary function

In the general problem of rolling–sliding, the complementary waves (pressures and clearance fluctuations) will decay in amplitude as they propagate in the contact due to the non-Newtonian effects from sliding. Hooke et al. [21] suggest an exponential decay respect to the inlet $( x ^ { \prime } = x + a )$ location. Besides, the complementary wave, since it propagates with the average speed of the lubricant, effectively will have a wavenumber in x such that $\omega _ { x } ^ { \prime } \approx \omega _ { x } ( u _ { 2 } / \bar { u } )$ . Assuming that the wave decays exponentially with distance at a rate $\beta ,$ the amplitude of the clearance and by consequence the pressure can be expressed as

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \delta h _ { \mathrm { c } } = h _ { \mathrm { c } } \exp ( i \psi x ^ { \prime } ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y ) } \\ { \delta p _ { \mathrm { c } } = p _ { c } \exp ( i \psi x ^ { \prime } ) \exp ( - i \omega _ { x } u _ { 2 } t ) \exp ( i \omega _ { y } y ) } \end{array}\tag{20}
$$

with $\psi = \omega _ { x } ^ { \prime } + i \beta$

Substituting these equations and the associated changes in density into the linearized Reynolds equation (16) and collecting the first-order terms, Hooke et al. [21] obtained,

$$
\psi = \omega _ { x } ^ { \prime } + i \frac { E ^ { \prime } h ^ { 3 } \sqrt { \psi ^ { 2 } + \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } } [ ( \psi ^ { 2 } / \eta _ { y } ) + ( \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } / \eta _ { y } ) ] } { 4 8 \bar { u } \left[ 1 + \left( E ^ { \prime } h \sqrt { \psi ^ { 2 } + \omega _ { y } ^ { 2 } } / ( 4 B ) \right) \right] }\tag{21}
$$

which needs to be solved for ψ at every roughness component. The numerical solution is possible by successively using the real part of equation (21) to estimate the real part of $\psi$ and the imaginary part to estimate the imaginary part of $\psi$

## 3.3 Amplitude of the complementary function

As mentioned before, the amplitude of the complementary function cannot be obtained only from the nominally parallel clearance assumption, here the inlet to the contact needs to be solved. Hooke et al. [21] proposed a curve-fit solution from numerically obtained perturbation solutions for every wave component ofthe roughness. In this paper, however, a different approach will be followed, because it is found to be simpler. Morales-Espejel et al. [14] used the numerically calculated amplitude reduction curves from Venner and Lubrecht [18] to approximate the amplitude of $h _ { \mathrm { c } }$ for Newtonian fluids as a function of the parameter. It follows a modification of this scheme for non-Newtonian fluids.

By solving numerically different sinusoidal patterns with Eyring fluid and rolling–sliding conditions, it is found that the amplitude reduction curves from Venner and Lubrecht can still be used but only with a new definition of the parameter , where the non-Newtonian effects are accommodated. This new parameter will be denoted as $\nabla _ { \mathrm { n n } }$ and it is defined as

$$
\nabla _ { \mathrm { n n } } = 0 . 8 \nabla \left( \frac { 1 + S } { 2 } \right) ^ { 0 . 1 + 0 . 5 K }\tag{22}
$$

with

$$
K = 1 - \operatorname { t a n h } \left( 0 . 2 5 { \frac { | Q | } { \nabla } } \right)\tag{23}
$$

thus

$$
h _ { \mathrm { t } } = { \frac { r _ { \mathrm { a } } } { 1 + 0 . 1 5 \nabla _ { \mathrm { n n } } + 0 . 0 1 5 \nabla _ { \mathrm { n n } } ^ { 2 } } }\tag{24}
$$

The clearance amplitude calculated with this scheme $h _ { \mathrm { t } }$ will include both components; therefore, the amplitude of the particular integral needs to be subtracted at every wave component to obtain $h _ { \mathrm { c } } ,$ leading to $h _ { \mathrm { c } } = h _ { \mathrm { t } } - h _ { \mathrm { a } }$ . This approximation is good enough in most cases as pointed out by C. J. Hooke (personal communication) when compared with the perturbation method. However, for truly universal calculations, the original method of Hooke et al. [21] should be used.

Finally, the complete pressures and clearance fluctuations are calculated by adding together the particular integral and the complementary function.

## 3.4 Other rheological models

As discussed by Hooke [20] and Hooke et al. [21] the scheme can easily be adapted to consider other rheological models different from Eyring. Different expressions for the equivalent viscosities (equation (19)) would need to be obtained, perhaps the only requirement is that they do not become zero (i.e. limiting shear stress model). Finally, the constants in equations (22) to (24) would probably need to be recalculated for the new rheology.

## 4 COMBINED MODEL

The model for the transition from boundary lubrication to full-film conditions combines the dry contact model described in section 3 and the full-film model described in section 4. Before showing the combination process, it is necessary to justify the use of the approach described in section 4 for the description of clearance and pressure fluctuations in conditions of very thin films and the transition to a dry contact solution.

The authors want to point out that they understand clearly that the Reynolds equation cannot be used to explain any film thickness break-down to produce dry contacts as long as the surface velocity remains different from zero and no lubricant-wall slippage is allowed. However, the present model only attempts to approximate the contact pressures and elastic deformation of the surface for the load sharing part that is important in friction. With this objective in mind, the authors believe that in very small clearance locations, it might result in less error to calculate these parameters using a dry contact model rather than a continuum-mechanics lubrication model. However, this question remains to be resolved and it is a matter of further discussions.

## 4.1 Justification for the use of the lubrication model in thin film conditions

The use of the scheme described in section 4 requires some attention given the simplifications made. In particular, the derivation of equation (16). This equation implies that the term in the Poiseuille flow $\rho h ^ { 3 } / ( 1 2 \eta )$ has nearly no variation respect to x (equal to its mean value), so it can be taken out of the derivative. Besides this, it is also assumed that the term in the Couette flow $h / B$ remains constant and equal to its mean value. These assumptions introduce some linearity in the Reynolds equation which allows its solution via the present method. Therefore, some care must be taken in order to remain within the limits of the approximation.

## 5.1 Comparison with numerical solutions from the literature

Deolalikar et al. [11] show solutions with the Newtonian lubricant for a spherical bump on a surface of an EHL contact making contact with the opposite surface. The geometry of the feature is schematically shown in Fig. 6. The height of the bump is 1.2 μm. The radius of the bump is estimated to be 18 μm from the graphs. The operating conditions given in the reference are summarized in Table 2.

Figure 7 shows a mid-plane profile ofthe solution for clearances and pressures given in reference [11], compared with the solution calculated with the present model. From the comparison, it can be said that the maximum pressure value with the present model is somehow lower than the value from the reference. However, this could be due to several factors: small differences in the mean clearance (in the present model the central film thickness is approximated with the smooth surface Hamrock–Dowson equation). Differences in the estimated bump radius $( 1 8 \mu \mathrm { m }$ and the actual value). Besides the reference mentions a mean film thickness five times smaller than the height of the bump. In the present model, a central film thickness about eight times smaller was calculated. In any case, the solutions are not too different for the maximum pressures.

Detailed comparisons show some important differences between the two solutions in Fig. 7. The reference shows a nearly symmetrical clearance around the contact spot and a slight loss of clearance in the leading edge of the deformed bump. On the contrary, the present solution shows an increase of clearance in the leading edge of the bump. The same increase can be seen in the pure-rolling measurements from Kaneta et al. [29] and the pure-rolling calculations from Hooke [30] and Hooke and Li [31] for transverse ridges and bumps. In fact, it is logical to expect that under pure rolling conditions the lubricant pocket entrapped by the surface feature at the inlet will simply be transported unchanged to the end of the contact. In Fig. 7, the solution from the reference shows as well the largest pressure drop in the leading edge of the bump, whereas the present solution shows this drop in the trailing edge, where the lubricant is prevented to go by the moving bump at the inlet. This drop of pressure can also be seen in pure-rolling fullnumerical and linearised calculations from Hooke [30] and Hooke and Li [31]. However, one has to keep in mind that in the present lubrication model there is no way to completely stop the lubricant flow when contact occurs, perhaps this effect is responsible of the local differences in clearances and pressures.

## 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

An engineering scheme has been presented for the calculation of deterministic micro-geometry effects in sliding friction for the transition zone between boundary and EHL regimes. The scheme applies concepts for micro-geometry deformations and pressures from hydrodynamics and dry contact. The present model requires the knowledge of the friction coefficients in fully boundary $( \mu _ { \mathrm { b l } } )$ and fully EHL $( \mu _ { \mathrm { e h l } } )$ regimes, the first one influenced by the tribochemistry ofthe lubricant-surface system and the second mainly determined by the rheology of the lubricant under the operating conditions. The present methodology, however, estimates the influence of the surface topography in the load distribution between the so-called dry contact spots and the lubricated areas $( \phi _ { \mathrm { b l } } )$ . The methodology is very fast (about 2 min in a PC for every traction curve with 12 speed intervals and a surface of 184 120 points).

The results from this engineering scheme have been confronted with numerical solutions from literature and with friction measurements from a ball-on-disc tribometer. Both comparisons show reasonable agreement with the present model, although some differences are present. In the described methodology, the load distribution in the transition area is predicted from a measured roughness sample. Ideally several samples of the rougher surface (the other is assumed smooth) should be measured in order to obtain a ‘representative’ sample ofthe running track, but variations from sample to sample will always exist. Therefore (contrary to the results presented here), a proper calculation of friction in these conditions should include some statistics of the variability of roughness in the raceway. Other source of variability not considered in the model but possibly present in the experiments is the actual central clearance (or film thickness) in the contact. It is well known that roughness, running-in, temperature, and other factors might influence this parameter. Certainly, at high sliding rates, thermal effects have to be considered in the sliding friction calculation. In the results presented here, no consideration of thermal effects has been made since the focus at this point is rolling bearings running at low speed.

The authors have used the term ‘engineering’ scheme or model because its contribution in providing new knowledge is modest. It is meant to help in the calculation of the load share (and friction) in the transition zone and does not attempt to describe the physics of the partial-lubrication regime. It is well known that under the assumptions of the Reynolds equation (including the linear form as presented here) it is impossible to predict real dry contact patches [12].

Therefore, from the above analysis, the following conclusions can be drawn.

1. An engineering scheme for the calculation of the load sharing between lubricant and dry spots in the transition regime for deterministic surfaces seems possible, by using the hydrodynamic deformations limited by the dry contact values. The scheme gives reasonable results when compared with numerical solutions and experiments.

2. The scheme is fast and it shows capabilities of capturing the effects of different deterministic topographies in sliding friction, once the friction coefficients in the boundary and full-film regimes are known.
